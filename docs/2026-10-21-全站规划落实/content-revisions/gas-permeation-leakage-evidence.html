Gas permeation through rubber and leakage through a joint or defect are different transport paths. Specify which question the test must answer before selecting a material or changing the assembly. A measured gas signal needs its method, conditions and interpretation; it is not automatically proof of one failure mechanism.

Identify the possible path

Leybold's vacuum-fundamentals guidance distinguishes permeation through materials from a leak associated with an opening or defect. It also distinguishes locating a leak from measuring a leak rate and emphasizes defined acceptance conditions.

Gas transport through the materialIdentified compound, gas, specimen geometry and a permeability-test method

Gas released from material or trapped spacesRelevant preparation, exposure and gas-release investigation rather than an assumed joint defect

The vacuum-outgassing guide addresses a separate contamination and gas-release task. Keep that evidence distinct when interpreting a vacuum-system result.

Read a permeability report by its measured quantity

ISO 2782-1 covers methods for determining gas permeability of rubber under a differential partial pressure. Its public scope distinguishes measurement outputs such as gas transmission and permeability-related coefficients according to the method.

Identify whether the report gives an actual flow or transmission rate, a normalized quantity or a material coefficient. Retain the full units and the method's definitions. A result that includes specimen thickness in its normalization cannot be compared with an unnormalized component flow by reading the numerical values alone.

Keep gas and specimen conditions together

Record gas identity and composition, pressure conditions, temperature and the compound and specimen being tested. Retain thickness, exposed area, preparation and the measurement interval where applicable to the method. Ask how the reported state was established and what uncertainty or detection limit matters to the decision.

For a mixture, identify the relevant gas components rather than substituting a generic “gas-resistant” description. A result for one compound and gas does not establish a universal ranking across polymer families or every service condition.

Define the assembled-seal acceptance test

Specify the mating geometry, seal dimensions, installation method and the test configuration. Identify gas, pressure direction, temperature, duration, measured output and acceptance limit. State whether the task is to measure the total assembly response or locate a suspected leak.

If a signal exceeds the criterion, preserve the part and installation evidence before changing material. Review what the method can distinguish and whether additional checks are required to identify the path. A lower-permeability material does not, by itself, correct an assembly defect.

Link material data to component evaluation

For candidate supply, compare material results under a common basis and agree the finished-part trial. The O-ring groove-design discussion provides dimensional inputs; the failure guide helps organize observations after a deviation.

Keep the approved compound, drawing and test requirements together for repeat orders. Any later change should be reviewed against the relevant material and assembly evidence rather than only against a family name or hardness value.

Send RubberQ the gas-service and acceptance requirements with the seal drawing and existing reports. Discuss seal specifications and the evaluation scope before using a permeation or leakage value in the procurement decision.
