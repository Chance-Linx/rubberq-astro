Rubber Tensile Curves: Read the Axes, Specimen and Test Conditions

Read a rubber tensile curve with its stress definition, strain definition, specimen and test conditions. A curve without that context is an incomplete basis for selecting a compound. The measured tensile response also needs to be separated from the geometry and loading of the finished component.

Start with the axes

Engineering stress uses force divided by the original cross-sectional area. Engineering strain uses the change in gauge length divided by the original gauge length. State whether strain is shown as a ratio or percentage and confirm which definitions the report uses.

By definition, an engineering strain of 100 percent corresponds to an increase in gauge length equal to the original length. This is a geometric definition, not a test result or a permissible design strain. Likewise, stress based on the original area should not be confused with a value based on the changing area during extension.

Distinguish the reported properties

Reported itemWhat to identify
Stress at a specified elongationThe stated elongation point, stress definition and units
Tensile strengthThe method's reported strength value and the specimen it represents
Elongation at breakThe strain definition and the recorded break event
Loading or unloading traceWhich path, cycle and prior conditioning are displayed
Repeat measurementsThe individual results, reported statistic and variability represented

If a report uses the term “modulus,” ask for its definition. A stress reported at a specified elongation and the slope of a curve are different quantities. Keep the named method and the definition with the number before comparing it with another material sheet.

Compare like specimens and conditions

The ASTM D412 public guidance identifies specimen geometry, extension rate, temperature, humidity and conditioning among the factors affecting tensile results. It also distinguishes specimen methods whose results are not identical.

Request the compound, lot, cure and preparation state, specimen type and dimensions, test temperature and extension conditions. If a specimen was cut from a finished part, document where it came from and how it was prepared. Those details determine what comparison the curve can support.

Do not invent universal phases for every rubber curve

Evaluate the actual trace rather than assigning fixed strain bands to all elastomers. Identify the measured behaviour and the conditions before proposing an explanation. If a mechanism is important to a design decision, request the evidence needed to assess it instead of using a polymer-family ranking as the explanation.

For repeat loading, state whether the displayed trace is the first loading, a later cycle or another defined state. A monotonic tensile-to-break result does not supply a fatigue-life result or a complete dynamic response.

Translate the material result into a component question

Bring the drawing, deformation, contacting media and temperature history to the review. State which function must be maintained and what acceptance method applies. A high elongation at break does not, on its own, specify a suitable repeated working deformation for the assembled part.

For a bonded component, retain the insert and interface requirements separately; the adhesion-report guide covers that different measurement task. For aging or fluid-exposure comparisons, specify whether tensile measurements are before or after the agreed exposure.
